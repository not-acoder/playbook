## Intent

Provide students with a visibly non-linear progression path where additional effort on optional micro-tasks yields XP that can offset weak performance, increasing engagement and distributing practice without forcing repeated retakes of major assessments.

## Problem

Traditional grading often concentrates weight in a few major assessments, which raises stakes and can discourage experimentation and iterative practice. Students may under-engage with ungraded or low-stakes materials, seeing little direct grade payoff. There is demand for systems that both preserve recognizable grades and expand ways for students to demonstrate learning and recover from setbacks.

## Solution

The course is divided into modules, each with required graded assessments shared by both control and experimental sections, and an additional suite of gamified activities. In the gamified section, students complete more, smaller assignments, lowering the impact of any single low grade. They can also earn XP by completing optional video-based quizzes: 5–10 videos per week are available, arranged in a fixed order where progression requires a perfect score on each preceding quiz, pushing students toward mastery-level performance on micro-tasks. XP from these activities adds directly to the final grade calculation, creating a second progression channel that rewards persistence and exploration.

## Applicability

Works well in content-heavy STEM courses where additional practice problems or concept videos can be easily created (e.g., statistics, introductory programming, engineering fundamentals). Effective in online and hybrid courses with LMS capabilities for chaining activities and tracking completion. Suited to courses where instructors want more frequent, low-stakes checks without replacing existing summative exams.

Tradeoffs/limitations: Requires significant upfront design time to create multiple small assignments and sequenced video quizzes. XP bonuses must be calibrated to avoid grade inflation and perceptions of "gaming the system" without learning. Students who cannot regularly access online content may be disadvantaged if XP has substantial grade impact.

Not a good fit when: Institutional or departmental policies tightly constrain grading formulas, leaving no room for additive XP-type bonuses. Courses rely almost exclusively on in-person, hands-on assessments that are hard to break into small online tasks.

## How to Implement

1. Define required graded components (e.g., exams, problem sets, labs) and weightings that would function as a complete course on their own.
2. Break some larger assignments into smaller, more frequent tasks to lower per-assignment stakes while keeping total workload comparable.
3. For each module, create 5–10 short videos or equivalent micro-tasks, each followed by a quiz that must be completed with a perfect score before unlocking the next, creating the chained mastery structure.
4. Decide how many XP students can earn and how XP translates into grade points (e.g., up to 5 percentage points addable to the final grade).
5. Use LMS tools to show students their current XP and how it affects their projected course grade, reinforcing the sense of progression.
6. If possible, run one section with the XP system and another with only the traditional structure, then compare engagement and course performance to evaluate impact.

## Examples

In the business course, the experimental section used more numerous, smaller assignments so that low scores had less impact, while the control section kept fewer, higher-stakes tasks, with all other course elements held constant.

Optional video quizzes offered students the chance to add points to their final grade; 5–10 videos per week were available, and each quiz had to be completed perfectly to unlock the next, ensuring close attention to content.

Gamified elements included leaderboards, badges, and interactive quizzes layered onto the same curriculum and learning objectives as the control group. Results indicated that the gamified section showed higher engagement and positive effects on performance-related outcomes.
