## Intent

Students use ePortfolios to curate artifacts and reflections that document their intellectual growth, identity as scientists or engineers, and engagement with equity-relevant STEM problems over time.

## Problem

Traditional exams and point-based assignments capture isolated performances but rarely show longitudinal growth, identity development, or integrative learning. This is especially limiting for underrepresented students whose evolving sense of belonging and competence in STEM is central to persistence but invisible in conventional grading schemes.

## Solution

The approach embeds ePortfolios as a graded, recurring assignment that requires students to collect artifacts (projects, lab work, concept maps, reflections) aligned with course and program outcomes. Prompts explicitly connect course content to civic issues, equity, and students' own goals, foregrounding their experiences and voices. The portfolio serves as a site for ongoing formative assessment and metacognitive growth through instructor and peer feedback on entries.

## Applicability

Works well in first-year STEM seminars, writing-intensive science courses, and lab courses where students can regularly reflect on experiences. Effective for programs seeking to integrate multiple high-impact practices (undergraduate research, writing-intensive work, civic engagement) in a coherent way.

Tradeoffs/limitations: Requires scaffolding of reflective writing and basic technology support for ePortfolio platforms. Instructors must commit to reading and responding to entries, which adds grading time.

Not a good fit when: Courses have almost no written or project-based work to upload. Students have very limited technology access and institutional support is minimal.

## How to Implement

1. Decide whether the ePortfolio will primarily evidence learning outcomes, support reflection, showcase professional identity, or combine these aims.
2. Create prompts that ask students to connect course content to real-world issues and to their own experiences (e.g., linking sanitation engineering concepts to global public health).
3. Specify what types of artifacts students should include (lab reports, projects, concept maps, discussion posts) and how they illustrate particular outcomes.
4. Make the ePortfolio a graded component using a rubric that values reflection quality, integration across experiences, and evidence of growth, not just aesthetics. Schedule checkpoints (e.g., three portfolio submissions during the term) and build time for feedback cycles.
5. Incorporate structured peer commenting on ePortfolio entries so students see diverse paths through the course and feel part of a learning community.

## Examples

First-year seminar "How the Toilet Changed the World": Students maintained weekly ePortfolio reflections on course readings, simple lab activities, and global sanitation issues, with prompts such as "What did you know about toilets before this course, and what surprised you this week?" Students reported that ePortfolio work significantly helped their learning (mean ratings around 3.7–4.3 on a 1–5 scale).

Non-major science course at a community college: Students in an experimental group submitted discussion responses and low-stakes tasks via ePortfolios and commented on peers; they showed higher engagement and course grades than students submitting work on paper.
