## Intent

Students define personalized growth goals for an independent project and use structured reflection and feedback cycles to document their development, with project grades emerging from demonstrated growth rather than accumulated points.

## Problem

Content-heavy STEM courses often bolt on independent research projects but still grade them with one high-stakes rubric at the end, which can penalize students who improve slowly or start with weaker preparation. Traditional grading on such projects can also undermine student agency, making the project feel like another assignment rather than a space to develop an identity as a scientist.

## Solution

The course uses a guided ungrading structure where students set individualized growth goals connected to core research skills and then iterate through multiple reflection, peer feedback, and instructor feedback checkpoints across the semester. Rather than receiving frequent numeric scores, students gather evidence (artifacts, reflections, feedback) demonstrating how they have progressed toward their self-defined goals, and this evidence is used to determine the project portion of their course grade.

## Applicability

Works well in upper-division or capstone STEM courses with substantial student-designed projects (e.g., lab research, software projects, design projects). Effective in small to medium seminar-style courses where instructors can provide in-class feedback and manage multiple checkpoints.

Tradeoffs/limitations: Requires significant investment in designing reflection prompts and facilitating peer review. Some students may initially feel uncertain without points and may seek instructor validation frequently.

Not a good fit when: Course enrollment is very large and project mentoring capacity is limited. Institutional policies require detailed point breakdowns for all components, leaving little flexibility.

## How to Implement

1. Identify key domains for the project (e.g., experimental design, data analysis, scientific writing, communication). Ask students to select and articulate a small set of growth goals in these domains, focusing on skills they most want or need to develop.
2. Design three structured reflection checkpoints across the project timeline where students submit written reflections on progress toward their goals. Incorporate two peer-review sessions (e.g., on written reports or oral presentations) where students give and receive feedback linked to their growth goals.
3. Reserve time during class meetings for the instructional team to circulate, ask questions about project progress, and offer formative feedback instead of assigning points.
4. At the end of the term, have students synthesize evidence (reflections, drafts, peer feedback, project artifacts) to argue how they met their growth goals. Use that portfolio of evidence to determine the project grade portion (e.g., 40% of course), while keeping other course components (quizzes, midterm) on conventional grading.

## Examples

The course used three structured reflection checkpoints and two peer-review sessions, plus regular in-class feedback from the instructional team during project work time.

The ungrading model applied only to the research project, which accounted for about 40% of the course grade; quizzes and a midterm exam remained traditionally graded.

Students with data science or computer science backgrounds sometimes set growth goals in subspecialties such as statistical approaches or data visualization, while others chose areas like scientific writing where they felt less confident.
