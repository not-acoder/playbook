## Intent

Incentivize collaboration, reliability, and positive contribution to peers' learning by integrating a quantified reputation component into the grade, derived from structured peer feedback with differential weighting.

## Problem

Group work and interactive activities often suffer from free-riding and uneven contributions, yet traditional grading either ignores these dynamics or handles them informally. Standard peer assessment typically influences grades indirectly or in ad-hoc ways, leading to concerns about fairness and transparency. There is a need for a systematic, formula-based approach that uses peer perceptions of contribution while minimizing arbitrary instructor discretion.

## Solution

At defined points in the course, students complete a Reputation Index form where they assign Good or Bad ratings to peers, indicating whether they have had direct experience working with each peer. The grading scheme assigns +2 points for each Good evaluation from a student with direct experience and +1 point for each Good evaluation from a student without direct experience; correspondingly, −2 and −1 points are assigned for Bad evaluations from direct and indirect raters. All points are summed to create an absolute reputation score per student, which is then linearly scaled to a fixed grade contribution (e.g., up to 20 points out of a larger total). Comments are anonymized and returned to students as feedback, with additional credit for positive "C votes" indicating contribution to learning.

## Applicability

Works well in STEM courses with significant group work or collaborative projects (e.g., design courses, lab-based courses, capstones) where peer interactions are central. Effective in seminar-style or project-based classes where instructors seek to formalize otherwise informal reputational judgments into a clear grade formula, and in courses aiming to cultivate teamwork, communication, and professional behaviors as explicit learning goals.

Tradeoffs/limitations: Requires careful explanation to students to avoid perceived popularity contests and ensure evaluations focus on contribution and behavior. Instructors must manage anonymity, potential bias, and the administrative load of collecting and converting evaluations into scores.

Not a good fit when: Courses are mostly individual and do not involve meaningful peer interaction or collaboration. Class culture or institutional context makes students uncomfortable with evaluating peers in a consequential way. There are strong concerns about bias or retaliation that cannot be mitigated through anonymity and clear guidelines.

## How to Implement

1. Allocate a specific number of points (e.g., 20 points) in the overall grading scheme for the Reputation Index, and describe the evaluation process and point conversion clearly in the syllabus.
2. Create a form where students list peers, indicate whether they have had direct experience working with each one, assign a Good or Bad rating, and provide a brief comment for each negative vote.
3. Adopt the weighting scheme: +2 points for a Good from a direct-experience rater, +1 point for a Good from a non-direct rater, −2 points for a Bad from a direct rater, and −1 point for a Bad from a non-direct rater.
4. After students submit evaluations, sum all positive and negative points per student to yield an absolute reputation score.
5. Map the observed range of absolute scores to the fixed grade contribution (e.g., 8–10 points for the lowest reputations up to 20 points for the highest).
6. Return each student's positive and negative sub-scores, total reputation score, final RI grade points, and anonymized comments; convert certain positive "C votes" (partners contributing to learning) into extra credit.

## Examples

In MG473, students must complete a Reputation Index, providing comments for every negative vote; these evaluations are then turned into numerical grades contributing up to 20 points.

A Good evaluation from someone with direct experience yields +2 points; a Good from someone without direct experience yields +1; corresponding Bad evaluations yield −2 and −1 points, respectively.

All "C votes" indicating that a partner contributed to the evaluator's learning, along with their comments, carry over as extra credit in the reputation mark, reinforcing pro-social behavior.
