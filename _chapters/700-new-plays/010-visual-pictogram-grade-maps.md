## Intent

Students develop an integrated understanding of their performance across dimensions, enabling them to spot patterns (for example, strong concepts but weak application) and make targeted changes.

## Problem

Many students interpret grades as opaque numbers and have difficulty seeing how different components of their performance relate to each other. Traditional gradebooks often obscure patterns such as uneven skills, non-cognitive strengths, or consistent issues across assignments, which limits students' ability to self-regulate and instructors' ability to diagnose systemic issues.

## Solution

This practice replaces conventional numeric grade columns with a pictogram-based representation of performance across multiple criteria, effectively turning the gradebook into a visual map. Each criterion (for example, conceptual understanding, problem-solving, collaboration) is associated with a small set of performance levels, and each level is represented by a distinct pictogram (different shapes, fills, or colors). For every student, these pictograms are arranged into a compact grid or card, with rows for criteria and columns for time points or assignment clusters, so a single view shows the student's achievement profile. Because the same icons are reused over time, students can quickly see where pictograms shift from lower to higher levels, making progress and persistent weaknesses visually salient.

## Applicability

Works well in medium-enrollment undergraduate STEM courses that already track several learning outcomes (for example, programming, lab-based physics, engineering design). Effective in courses or programs that define explicit competency dimensions (such as ABET outcomes, lab skills, modeling, reasoning) with a small number of performance levels, and in learning environments that want more holistic progress reports.

Tradeoffs/limitations: Requires up-front work to define performance levels and design pictograms that are clear and distinguishable for students. Students accustomed to points and percentages may initially find the representation unfamiliar and need orientation. Creating and maintaining visual grade maps may demand tools or workflows beyond a default LMS gradebook.

Not a good fit when: A course has very few assessments and no meaningful sub-dimensions to represent visually. Institutional systems demand that all communication about grades be strictly numeric. Instructors lack time or support to design and refine visual encodings with students.

## How to Implement

1. Identify 4–6 core performance dimensions that matter in the course (for example, conceptual understanding, procedure execution, modeling, collaboration, reflection) and define 3–4 clearly described performance levels for each.
2. Design a simple, distinct pictogram for each performance level (for instance, different shapes or fill patterns), and create a one-page legend that students can reference.
3. For each student, construct a visual grid or card where rows are criteria and columns are either major assignments or checkpoints; after each checkpoint, place the appropriate pictogram in each cell.
4. Use the pictogram maps in feedback conversations and progress emails, asking students first to interpret their visual profile before discussing numbers.
5. Periodically review a sample of maps across the class to identify common patterns (for example, many students at low levels on error analysis) and adjust instruction accordingly.
6. Collect brief student feedback on whether the pictograms help them understand and act on their performance, and refine icon design or layout in response.

## Examples

In the original context, pictograms were used to encode multiple aspects of student performance into a compact representation, allowing educators to assess and communicate achievement across many criteria without relying solely on numeric scores.

A STEM adaptation might define rows for "experimental technique," "data representation," "error analysis," and "conceptual explanation" in a lab course, with each lab producing a new column of pictograms so students can see improvement or stagnation over the term.

In a programming course, columns could correspond to milestones (loops, arrays, recursion, data structures), and students would see at a glance that their "test coverage" pictogram lags behind "correctness" across several milestones, prompting targeted practice.
