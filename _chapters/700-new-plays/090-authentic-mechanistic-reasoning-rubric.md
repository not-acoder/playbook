## Intent

Students demonstrate understanding by constructing mechanistic explanations using structured written reasoning, with grading aligned to authentic chemical practice instead of short, algorithmic answer formats.

## Problem

Traditional chemistry exams often emphasize quick algorithmic problem solving and pattern recognition, which can advantage students with strong test-taking experience while under-valuing deeper conceptual understanding. These designs may exacerbate performance gaps between demographic groups even when conceptual capabilities are similar.

## Solution

The course replaces many algorithmic items with tasks that require students to generate mechanistic explanations for chemical phenomena (e.g., electron-pushing mechanisms) instead of just final answers. Students respond to prompts structured around claims, evidence, and reasoning, which are graded using detailed rubrics targeting mechanistic reasoning quality and completeness. They receive feedback on their mechanistic reasoning, reinforcing the kinds of thinking valued in the discipline.

## Applicability

Works well in chemistry courses where mechanisms and causal explanations are core (e.g., organic or physical chemistry) and in analogous fields (e.g., physics reasoning, CS algorithmic justification). Effective in courses willing to redesign exam questions and rubrics to emphasize reasoning rather than rapid calculation.

Tradeoffs/limitations: Creating high-quality mechanistic prompts and rubrics requires time and content expertise. Grading written reasoning can be slower than grading short-answer items, particularly in large classes.

Not a good fit when: Courses where the primary learning goals are basic procedural skills with little emphasis on explanatory reasoning. Settings without grader capacity to apply rubrics reliably.

## How to Implement

1. Determine which disciplinary practices (e.g., drawing and explaining mechanisms, justifying algorithm design choices) the assessments should foreground.
2. Convert existing exam questions into prompts that ask for mechanistic explanations, including explicit requests for reasoning steps and justifications.
3. Create rubrics that describe levels of performance on key dimensions such as correctness of mechanism, coherence of reasoning, and appropriate use of representations.
4. Use the rubric to score exam responses, and provide rubric-based feedback so students see how to strengthen their mechanistic reasoning.
5. Compare performance distributions and gaps across demographic groups under the new assessments versus prior cohorts to see if inequities are reduced.

## Examples

Exams emphasized mechanistic reasoning as the "intellectual work" authentic to the practice of chemistry, with rubric criteria aimed at assessing students' ability to construct and communicate mechanisms rather than quickly compute numeric answers.

Analyses showed that this shift in assessment design influenced who succeeded in the course, with implications for reducing inequities tied to traditional algorithmic exams.
