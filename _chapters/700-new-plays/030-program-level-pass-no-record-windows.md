## Intent

Students use an early segment of their program to explore disciplines and build foundational skills without long-term GPA penalties, while still receiving meaningful feedback to guide decisions about preparation and major choice.

## Problem

High-stakes grading in the first year can discourage students from exploring challenging fields and amplify stress and inequities, because early missteps are permanently baked into GPAs and transcripts. Isolated course-level grading changes do little to alter this structural issue; what is needed is a mechanism that reshapes how grades function across an entire segment of the curriculum.

## Solution

A program-level pass/no-record (P/NR) window reframes grades in the first year from permanent record to exploratory feedback. In this structure, passing work in designated terms appears on the external transcript simply as a pass, with no letter grade or GPA impact, while instructors still record letter grades internally that are visible to students and advisors. The policy can be extended to cover more units and a broader range of subjects, including some early major courses, thereby lowering the perceived risk of trying new fields while preserving rigorous evaluation. Evaluation at MIT showed that an expanded P/NR framework increased flexibility in first-year course selection and supported exploration, while faculty maintained expectations because internal letter grades continued to be used for feedback and advising.

## Applicability

Works well in STEM schools or colleges with a demanding common first-year curriculum and substantial advising infrastructure. Effective for institutions looking to encourage cross-discipline exploration and reduce first-year performance anxiety without abandoning standards. Best suited for programs where centralized academic policy changes are feasible and where registrars can implement dual recording (internal letter grades and external P/NR).

Tradeoffs/limitations: Implementing the policy requires institution-level coordination across departments, the registrar, and advising offices. Some stakeholders may worry that transcripts with many P/NR entries convey less information to external audiences such as graduate schools or employers. Students must still be encouraged to take internal letter grades seriously.

Not a good fit when: External regulations or accreditation requirements strictly dictate transcript formats and do not permit P/NR-like notations. The advising system is too thin to help students interpret internal letter grades. Programs are small and highly specialized with limited elective space.

## How to Implement

1. Decide which terms (for example, first semester or first academic year) and what share of credits will fall under P/NR, and draft policy language describing how passes and non-records will appear on external transcripts.
2. Configure grading and registrar systems so that instructors continue to assign letter grades, but students see both the internal letter grade (for feedback) and the external P/NR outcome for covered courses.
3. Identify foundational and exploratory courses that can reasonably be taken under P/NR without disrupting prerequisites, and publish an approved list with clear guidance.
4. Integrate the P/NR policy into first-year advising, explaining that internal letter grades should guide decisions about repeating material, course sequencing, and readiness for upper-level work.
5. Communicate expectations to faculty that grading standards remain unchanged and that internal letter grades should reflect actual performance to preserve meaningful feedback.
6. Monitor effects on course enrollment patterns, progression into majors, and student-reported stress and exploration experiences, and adjust the scope or rules of the P/NR window based on evidence.

## Examples

At MIT, an experiment expanded P/NR beyond the traditional structure so that more first-year subjects, including some in prospective majors, could be taken without immediate GPA consequences, while letter grades continued to be recorded internally.

A college of engineering could designate the first two required math and science courses as P/NR for external reporting, allowing students to attempt more challenging sequences while advisors use internal letter grades to recommend whether to repeat or advance.

A computing program might allow exploratory P/NR enrollment in introductory data science, robotics, or cybersecurity courses so that students can test interest without fear that a single misaligned course choice will harm their GPA.
