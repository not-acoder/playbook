## Intent

Students can revise and resubmit formative assessments without penalty until they demonstrate mastery, with flexible deadlines that extend beyond initial due dates.

## Problem

Students enter courses with varying levels of prior experience. Those who have learned a subject previously are better equipped to succeed on weekly assignments, while those new to a subject are still learning how to put pieces together and are more likely to make mistakes as they learn.

Formative assessments should be opportunities for students to interact with new material and explore how everything fits together—activities like weekly labs, quizzes, practice programs, and certain exams. Making mistakes or failing to comprehend a subject's complexity are common situations, and students should not be penalized for these as they advance toward proficiency.

Unfortunately, traditional grading models frequently grade weekly work based on overall correctness and penalize students who are making legitimate progress but have not yet reached mastery.

## Solution

Flexible grading of formative assessments makes grades more equitable and promotes a growth mindset through two main mechanisms:

**Flexible Deadlines**: An initial deadline is announced for all formative assessments. Students who miss this deadline may be reminded of outstanding activities but are not penalized. Students are actively encouraged to submit missing materials up until a "final" deadline, often at course end but potentially earlier for practical reasons. The timeframe between initial and final deadlines should be long enough to accommodate students who fall behind but make legitimate effort to catch up.

**Multiple Attempts**: Formative assessments assess progress on learning. When done properly, they help students identify lingering gaps in their knowledge or understand when activities aren't completed as expected. When gaps are identified, students are encouraged to revise and resubmit until they get assignments "right."

Assignments can be submitted for initial assessment and may have conditions before a redo/retake attempt is allowed. All formative work is understood to be for learning purposes and should offer opportunities to further student learning by revisiting mistakes or improving progress.

## Applicability

**When this works well:**

Revising and resubmitting is most applicable when activities have elements of creativity, personal style, or narrative—code, short answers, essays, and similar work. In these situations, understanding why an answer is wrong or insufficient involves more than learning the "correct" answer and requires renewed effort and learning from students.

**Challenges to consider:**

**Grading workload**: Allowing revisions and resubmissions requires additional effort from teaching personnel who must continue grading assignments over multiple weeks and maintain consistency in grades and feedback across submissions. This can be challenging in large classes or courses with significant variability in response types and quality.

**Creating conditions for revision**: You may need to establish requirements students must meet before revising (meeting with faculty/TA, identifying what changed in their submission, completing related preparatory work). These conditions help ensure students engage meaningfully with feedback rather than making superficial changes.

**Balancing flexibility with progress**: While flexible deadlines support diverse student needs, some structure helps students make adequate progress. The source paper's implementation combined flexible deadlines with mechanisms to prevent excessive procrastination, such as blocking access to new material until students completed current week's work.

## How to Implement

**1. Establish submission workflows**

Define clear processes for three submission types:

**On-time first attempts**: Standard submission through your LMS or grading system by the initial deadline.

**Late first attempts**: Same submission process but after the initial deadline and before the final deadline. Make clear these receive full credit without penalty.

**Revised work**: May use the same submission mechanism or a separate resubmission portal. Clearly indicate this is a revision of previous work (some systems allow version tracking).

**2. Create grading rubrics for consistency**

Develop rubrics or grading mechanisms that help maintain consistency across multiple submissions and different graders. The source paper used a 0-4 point scale with growth mindset language:
- **0 - Learning**: Significant gaps remain
- **1 - Approaching**: Making progress but not yet proficient
- **2 - Meets**: Demonstrates expected proficiency
- **3 - Exceeds**: Goes beyond expectations

This scale applies consistently whether grading initial submissions or revisions.

**3. Set revision conditions strategically**

Determine what (if anything) students must do before resubmitting:

**For simple corrections**: May allow immediate resubmission once feedback is provided.

**For significant revisions**: May require students to:
- Meet with a TA or instructor to discuss feedback
- Submit a brief explanation of what they changed and why
- Complete related practice problems or tutorials
- Demonstrate understanding of foundational concepts through quizzes or check-ins

The source paper required students to achieve mastery on related readings, knowledge checks, and labs before retaking exams—ensuring students studied between attempts rather than simply guessing again.

**4. Communicate the system clearly to students**

At course start, explain:
- Formative assessments are for learning; mistakes are expected and won't be punished
- Initial deadlines exist to keep students on pace but missing them isn't penalized
- Final deadlines (course end or earlier) after which no submissions accepted
- Any conditions that must be met before revisions allowed
- How this system supports their learning and growth mindset

**Remind students frequently** that revision is available—this may be a new concept and they need reinforcement that it's genuinely encouraged, not just theoretically permitted.

**5. Encourage revisions actively**

Don't assume students will automatically take advantage of revision opportunities:

**Send reminders**: Email students who have outstanding formative work or could improve their scores through revision.

**Track and display progress**: Show students their current scores and potential scores if they revised specific assignments.

**Celebrate improvement**: Highlight stories (anonymously) of students who substantially improved through revision.

**Address growth mindset**: Explicitly connect the revision process to growth mindset principles—the idea that ability grows through effort and learning from mistakes.

The source paper found that 65% of students increased their grades by at least 10 points (a letter grade) through resubmissions, and that students continued to revise even ungraded assignments when they contributed to learning.

**6. Manage the grading workload**

**Use auto-grading where possible**: For coding labs, quizzes with objective answers, and similar work, autograders can handle multiple submissions efficiently.

**Implement liberal drop policies**: Drop the lowest formative assignment score(s) to reduce pressure on both students and graders. Students don't need to master every single formative to demonstrate proficiency.

**Stagger due dates**: Spread formative assessments across the semester so revisions don't all arrive simultaneously.

**Set final deadlines strategically**: While the final deadline can be at course end, consider earlier cutoffs for specific assignments to manage workflow.
