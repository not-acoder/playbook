## Intent

Shift the pedagogical focus from point accumulation to a continuous cycle of high-quality feedback and student reflection, enabling learners to iteratively assess their progress and take ownership of their academic development.

## Problem

True learning occurs when students engage in critical thinking and meaningfully change their behavior or understanding. However, traditional point-based grading often obscures the feedback necessary for this growth. When students are primarily concerned with their numerical standing, they may view comments as justifications for a grade rather than actionable guidance. This fixation on outcomes can stifle risk-taking and prevent students from developing the metacognitive skills needed to self-correct and advance toward mastery.

## Solution

Implement a bidirectional feedback model where students and educators act as co-creators of knowledge. This approach integrates various feedback streams—including personal dialogue between student and instructor, peer-evaluations, and class-wide reviews—to ensure that assessment is "process-oriented, personal, and genuine." By providing frequent opportunities for students to reflect on their learning process, instructors can move away from being sole judges of performance and instead become mentors who guide students through a reflective loop of action, feedback, and refinement.

## Applicability

This play is essential for any course that aims to foster high-level critical thinking, such as art history, philosophy, or advanced science seminars. It is particularly beneficial in equitable grading models that allow for resubmissions, as it provides the necessary roadmap for improvement. However, instructors must consider the significant tradeoff of an increased workload, as providing qualitative, iterative feedback is more time-consuming than traditional marking. It works best in environments where a "pedagogy of care" is established, allowing students to feel secure while exploring challenging concepts and acknowledging their own learning gaps.

## How to Implement

Start by designing a sequence of 6–8 reflective assignments per term, ranging from cursory check-ins to intensive self-evaluations. Provide students with specific prompts that require them to assess their progress relative to course outcomes, their willingness to apply received feedback, and the intellectual risks they have taken. Integrate regular peer-review sessions to diversify the sources of feedback and build a collaborative classroom culture. To model growth, incorporate anonymous student feedback cycles where you adjust your own teaching based on their input. Finally, conclude the semester with an individual "grade conference" where students present their reflective portfolio and discuss their final achievement level, ensuring that the feedback loop culminates in a transparent and mutually agreed-upon evaluation of their mastery.
