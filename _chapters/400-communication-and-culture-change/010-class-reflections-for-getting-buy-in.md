## Intent

Students develop buy-in for equitable grading practices through structured reflection activities that help them examine their own experiences with grades and learning.

## Problem

Equitable grading practices are often unfamiliar to students and administrators, which may conflict with deeply-held beliefs about traditional grading. Students entering courses with equitable grading may feel uncertain or skeptical because these approaches differ from what they've experienced throughout their educational careers. Without understanding the rationale behind alternative grading practices, students may resist the approach or struggle to engage fully with the learning process.

Changing mindsets about grading is crucial for successful implementation. If students don't understand why an instructor chose a particular grading approach, they may focus their energy on navigating an unfamiliar system rather than on learning.

## Solution

Use student-centered discussions and reflection activities that enable students to examine their own experiences with grades and learning, arriving at their own conclusions about the relationship between the two. Rather than telling students why equitable grading is valuable, create space for them to discover this through guided reflection.

Key components include:
- Structured activities where students explore associations with "grades" versus "learning"
- Emphasis on student proficiency and learning as the course goal, not grade achievement
- Transparent acknowledgment of initial discomfort students may experience
- Reframing language from "getting good grades" to "achieving proficiency or mastery"

## Applicability

This approach is broadly applicable across educational contexts. Regardless of discipline or level, students benefit from understanding not only *how* grades will be determined but *why* the instructor chose their grading approach.

**Particularly valuable when:**
- Students are more familiar with traditional grading approaches (common in higher education)
- Implementing ungrading, specifications grading, contract grading, or other alternative systems
- Working with students who have experienced grade-focused environments throughout their education
- The course uses substantially different assessment than students expect

**Consider the investment:** This play requires class time for reflection activities and discussion. The payoff is increased student engagement and reduced resistance, but instructors must weigh this against other uses of class time.

## How to Implement

**1. Design a reflection activity for early in the term**

Introduce equitable grading practices during the first week of class through structured reflection. One effective approach from the source:

**Word association activity:**
- Ask students to freely associate with the word "grade" - what comes to mind? Write responses on the board or collect them digitally.
- Ask students to freely associate with the word "learning" - what comes to mind? Again record responses.
- Facilitate discussion of the trends observed. Often, "grade" associations include anxiety, competition, judgment, stress, ranking, while "learning" associations include growth, discovery, understanding, skills, improvement.
- Guide students to recognize the disconnect: if learning is our goal, do grades support that goal based on these associations?

This activity provides a pathway for students to reach their own realizations about traditional grading's limitations rather than being told by the instructor.

**2. Be transparent about the discomfort**

Acknowledge explicitly that equitable grading will feel uncomfortable initially. Students have spent years learning to navigate traditional grading systems, so unfamiliarity creates anxiety.

Frame this proactively: "This will feel strange at first because it's different from what you're used to. That discomfort doesn't mean the system isn't working - it means you're adjusting to a new approach. By the end of the semester, most students find they've become comfortable with it and gained deeper appreciation for their own learning."

**3. Shift language throughout the course**

Consistently use language that emphasizes learning over grades:
- Replace "get good grades" with "achieve proficiency" or "demonstrate mastery"
- When students make mistakes, say "try another approach" instead of pointing out errors for deduction
- Frame assignments as opportunities to learn rather than opportunities to earn points

This linguistic shift reinforces the philosophical foundation of equitable grading.

**4. Provide ongoing opportunities for students to voice concerns**

Create mechanisms for students to share feedback about the grading system:
- Anonymous mid-semester surveys asking what's working and what's confusing
- Regular check-ins during class about the process
- Office hours focused on helping students understand the system

Address common concerns as they arise, refining your approach based on student input.

**5. Connect the system to student empowerment**

Help students see that equitable grading returns power to them. In the source implementation, students appreciated having agency in assigning their own grades. Frame the system as one that trusts students and values their self-assessment rather than one that judges them from above.
