## Intent

Use structured class reflections and word association activities to help students realize the limitations of traditional grades, thereby building the necessary buy-in for a shift toward growth-based and equitable assessment models.

## Problem

Students and administrators often hold deeply ingrained beliefs that grades are the primary, if not only, valid measure of academic success. This performance-oriented mindset can create significant resistance when an instructor introduces unfamiliar, equitable practices like ungrading or specifications grading. Without addressing these underlying values, students may feel anxious or confused, perceiving the new system as less rigorous or less fair than the traditional hierarchy they have navigated throughout their education.

## Solution

Facilitate student-centered discussions that allow learners to interrogate their own experiences with assessment. By contrasting the concepts of "grades" and "learning," instructors can lead students to conclude that traditional marks often act as a distraction from genuine mastery. Transparency is key: instructors should openly acknowledge that moving away from conventional points will feel uncomfortable at first. Affirming this discomfort while emphasizing the long-term benefits of intrinsic motivation and personal growth helps reframe the classroom as a collaborative community focused on skill development rather than a competitive environment focused on ranking.

## Applicability

This play is highly applicable in upper-division courses or subjects where critical thinking and synthesis are prioritized over rote memorization. It is especially valuable in institutions where traditional grading is the norm, as it provides a necessary pedagogical bridge for students transitioning to more equitable models. A major tradeoff is the increased emotional and time investment required from the instructor to manage student anxiety and provide intensive feedback. It works best when students feel safe to be honest about their prior academic struggles and when the instructor can provide data-driven evidence (e.g., historical grade averages) to alleviate fears of "grade inflation" or "rigor loss."

## How to Implement

At the beginning of the term, lead a "Free Association" activity where students list words they associate with "grade" versus "learning." Facilitate a class-wide discussion on the resulting trends, guiding students to realize where grades may have hindered their own curiosity or growth in the past. Assign regular reflective journals (roughly 6–8 per semester) that prompt students to assess their own progress relative to course learning objectives. Throughout these reflections, explicitly state that "failing is a part of learning" and reassure students that their end-of-term grade will be determined through a collaborative conference where they will present evidence of their mastery. Conclude the term with individual 10–15 minute "grade conferences" where students suggest their own final mark based on their reflective portfolio, ensuring they feel seen and valued as co-creators of their academic record.
