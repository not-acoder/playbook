## Intent

Establish the organizational and cultural conditions necessary for successful, large-scale grading reform by prioritizing teacher autonomy, collaborative leadership, and shared assessment frameworks over top-down mandates.

## Problem

Grading is a deeply personal activity that reflects an individual instructor's core values and pedagogical beliefs. When institutions attempt to impose standardized grading reforms from the top down, they often encounter significant resistance from educators who feel their professional autonomy is being undermined. This misalignment can lead to "grade grubbing," inconsistent student experiences across different sections of the same course, and a failure to sustain meaningful reform beyond the initial implementation phase.

## Solution

Create a bottom-up reform structure that empowers teachers as leaders and co-designers of the new system. This model relies on "course-alike" agreements where instructors teaching the same subject collaborate to define what counts toward a grade and how mastery will be measured. By providing dedicated time for data analysis and professional development—rather than issuing strict mandates—institutions can foster a sense of collective ownership. Essential conditions include establishing a minimum 50% floor for failing grades to maintain student hope, implementing robust test retake policies, and separating behavioral marks (like "citizenship" or effort) from academic mastery on report cards.

## Applicability

This play is specifically designed for large educational organizations, such as school districts or university departments with multiple sections of foundational courses. It is highly effective for improving consistency and reducing failure rates in "gateway" subjects like Algebra or introductory sciences. A primary tradeoff is the significant time and funding required to support teacher leaders and facilitate frequent collaborative meetings. It works best in environments where administrators are willing to suspend traditional mandates in favor of a value-based approach that emphasizes student achievement and teacher trust.

## How to Implement

Start by identifying "course leads"—veteran teachers or early adopters who can act as champions for reform within their departments. Provide these leads with dedicated funding and time to facilitate regular "course-alike" meetings focused on aligning curriculum and assessments. Avoid issuing mandates; instead, put specific reforms "on the table" for discussion, such as a 50% minimum F policy or a no-penalty late work policy. Utilize tools like high-speed scanners to provide immediate feedback on common assessments, freeing up meeting time for teachers to collaboratively analyze results and adjust instructional strategies. Finally, revise institutional reporting tools to separate academic grades from behavioral metrics, ensuring that the final mark is a transparent reflection of student knowledge rather than a measure of compliance.
