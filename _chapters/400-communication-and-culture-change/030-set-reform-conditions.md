## Intent
Create organizational conditions that support instructor buy-in and successful implementation of grading reforms across multiple courses, departments, or institutions.

## Problem
Grading practices reflect deeply held individual instructor beliefs about education, learning, and the purpose of schooling. When district or institutional leaders propose grading reforms without attending to these beliefs, instructors may resist changes they perceive as top-down mandates that undermine their professional autonomy and judgment. Even well-intentioned reforms can fail when instructors view them as conflicting with their values about preparing students for "the real world," rewarding effort, or maintaining academic standards. This resistance appears regardless of teaching experience—both veteran and newer teachers express concerns when reforms challenge their established grading philosophies.

## Solution
This play establishes three organizational conditions that create space for grading reform discussions while respecting instructor autonomy:

**Distributed Teacher Leadership:** Identify and empower teacher-leaders (department chairs, course leads, early adopters of alternative grading) to facilitate the reform process. These leaders organize workshops, collect data on current practices, lead discussions about proposed changes, and help design implementation approaches that fit their departments.

**Common Assessments and Curricular Alignment:** Develop shared assessments and pacing charts within course-alike groups. This creates a foundation for discussing what grades should represent and establishes consistency in "what counts" across sections of the same course.

**Dedicated Collaboration Time:** Provide structured time for teachers to discuss grading philosophies, examine student data, debate proposed reforms, and analyze implementation results. This allows reforms to be thoroughly discussed and refined based on instructor expertise and student outcomes.

These conditions work together: teacher leadership guides the conversation, common assessments provide concrete artifacts to discuss, and dedicated time enables meaningful dialogue about values and practices.

## Applicability
This approach works when:
- Leadership can commit resources for teacher leadership positions and collaboration time
- There is willingness to avoid mandates and allow reforms to emerge through discussion
- The organization has or can develop common assessments as discussion starting points
- Leaders can tolerate variation in implementation across departments or courses as teachers experiment

This approach may not work when:
- Timeline pressures require immediate, uniform implementation
- Resources for teacher leadership and collaboration time are unavailable
- The organization lacks trust between administration and teachers
- Subject areas have such different assessment needs that common structures don't make sense

**Tradeoffs:** This gradual, discussion-based approach takes significant time (the source district worked on reforms for 2+ years). It may result in uneven implementation across departments. However, it builds deeper buy-in and produces reforms informed by instructor expertise about what works in their contexts.

## How to Implement

**Phase 1: Plant Seeds and Build Knowledge**
Introduce grading reform concepts through reading materials and external expertise. The source district distributed "The Case Against the Zero" and *How to Grade for Learning* to core subject teachers, then brought in experts like Doug Reeves for workshops. This exposure-before-discussion approach lets teachers encounter ideas without immediate pressure to adopt them.

**Phase 2: Engage Teacher Leaders**
Hold focused meetings with teacher leaders (department chairs, course leads) about grading practices. Use these meetings to surface current practices, concerns, and potential interest in reforms. The source district conducted web-based surveys and focus groups with teacher leaders to understand existing grading philosophies and identify early adopters.

**Phase 3: Create Discussion Opportunities**
Provide multiple venues for grading discussions: district-wide in-service days with teacher-led workshops, department meetings, course-alike meetings. Frame reforms as "on the table" for discussion rather than mandates. The source district repeatedly used the phrase "keep hope alive" to connect grading reforms to their broader equity mission while maintaining that these were collaborative discussions, not requirements.

**Phase 4: Support Course-Alike Agreements**
Within departments or course-alike groups, facilitate development of shared grading policies. Start with common assessments (often already in place) as the foundation, then discuss what these assessments should mean for grades. The source district found that common assessments naturally led to conversations about consistency: "Before we had common assessments...students would complain that if they got teacher A, they could get an A, but if they had teacher B, they'd only get a C."

**Phase 5: Allow Experimentation and Iteration**
Let early adopter groups pilot reforms and share results. Use collaboration time to examine data from these pilots. The source district found that disconnects between student grades and state assessment results motivated some departments to examine their grading practices: "When we looked at the data and the CST scores had risen quite dramatically, and our grades didn't reflect that...that's when I think we really looked at alternative grading practices."

**Key Implementation Principles:**
- Avoid mandates; position reforms as collaborative exploration
- Connect grading reform to broader institutional values (e.g., equity, student success)
- Expect and accept variation in implementation across departments
- Provide concrete examples but let teachers adapt to their contexts
- Use data (student outcomes, grading patterns) to inform discussions without weaponizing it

## Examples

**Example 1: Teacher Leadership Structure**
The source district created "course lead" positions funded to support department chairs. Course leads facilitated course-alike meetings that included analyzing common assessment results, curriculum planning, and sharing instructional strategies. When the district wanted to explore grading reforms, these course leads became the primary discussion facilitators and implementation supporters, leveraging their existing relationships and credibility with colleagues.

**Example 2: Common Assessment Foundation**
An Algebra I team in the source district administered 14 common assessments per year. These shared assessments created natural conversations about grading: if students took the same tests, shouldn't they receive similar grades for similar performance? The team developed a course-alike agreement: 70% tests (including common assessments and final exam), 30% teacher discretion. They also agreed students could retake any test with the highest score counting, 50% was the minimum score on tests, and late work was accepted without penalty by 6 of 7 teachers.

**Example 3: Using Collaboration Time for Values Discussion**
When one department discussed the 50% minimum F policy, a veteran teacher initially resisted: "If a kid's not going to prepare for the exam, why should he be rewarded with getting a higher grade?" Through discussion in collaboration meetings, he reconsidered: "Then I thought about it; it's still an F. It's still 50%, and it does give the kid the opportunity to get out of the basement." This shift came through dialogue with colleagues, not mandate from administration.

**Example 4: Subject-Specific Adaptation**
A biology teacher in the source district implemented reforms differently than the math department. Rather than following his department's course-alike grading agreement, he assigned homework but didn't grade it, gave daily standards-based quizzes that constituted 100% of the grade, allowed retesting, and changed course grades based on end-of-year state test performance. He shared this approach at a district in-service. While he didn't gain many converts, his experimentation was supported as part of the broader exploration of grading practices.
