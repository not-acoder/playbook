## Intent

Combine competency-based grading, equitable assessment principles, and Universal Design for Instruction (UDI) to provide a transparent, multi-modal, and self-paced evaluation system for collegiate computer science courses.

## Problem

Traditional CS grading often relies on a "one size fits all" model with rigid deadlines and high-stakes exams. This system fails to account for students' diverse backgrounds, learning rates, and varying abilities to express knowledge in specific formats (e.g., written tests vs. verbal explanation). Such rigid structures can encourage academic dishonesty, where students prioritize "correct" output over conceptual understanding, and can foster a fixed mindset that penalizes early struggles.

## Solution

Implement a hybrid model built around 6–8 clearly defined core competencies. Students demonstrate proficiency through multiple assessment mechanisms—including 1-on-1 code reviews, oral interviews, randomized online quizzes, and written or video-based reports. This multi-modal approach aligns with UDI principles by allowing students to play to their strengths. The system supports a growth mindset by permitting multiple attempts at any competency without penalty until the end of the term. Performance is measured on a 5-point scale (Mastery, Approaching Mastery, Proficiency, Basic, Inconclusive), and final grades are determined by a transparent mapping table based on the level achieved across all competencies.

## Applicability

This play is most effective in small-to-medium CS courses (25–50 students) where staffing is adequate to support frequent 1-on-1 interactions. It is particularly well-suited for upper-level courses where students have developed the executive function to manage a self-paced workload. For introductory courses (CS1), instructors should consider adding structural scaffolding—such as weekly "engagement" competencies or designated "Evaluation Days"—to prevent procrastination and ensure students stay on track. A significant tradeoff is the increased instructor time spent on 1-on-1 assessments, though this is often offset by a reduction in traditional manual grading.

## How to Implement

Start by identifying the 6–8 essential skills students must master to succeed in future courses. Design at least three different ways for students to demonstrate each competency (e.g., a lab task, an oral quiz, and a written reflection). To manage your workload, set up a scheduling system for 15-minute 1-on-1 check-ins and enforce a "one attempt per day" rule to prevent end-of-semester "cramming." Dedicate specific days in the syllabus as "Evaluation Days" where no new lecture content is presented, allowing all students to focus on assessment. When a student attempts a competency, provide immediate qualitative feedback and assign a level on the 5-point scale. Maintain a public (but anonymous) or individual tracking sheet that shows students exactly how many competencies they need at each level to reach their desired final grade (e.g., an 'A' requires 4 Masteries and 4 Approaching Masteries).
