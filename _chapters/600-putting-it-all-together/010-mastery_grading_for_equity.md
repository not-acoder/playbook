## Intent
Grade students based on demonstrated mastery of learning outcomes rather than points accumulated across assignments.

## Problem
Traditional grading systems create several equity issues. First, they often conflate content mastery with behaviors like attendance, participation, and meeting deadlines—rewarding students who already know how to "do school" rather than those who understand course material. Second, point-based systems using 0-100% scales create mathematical distortions: a single zero can require multiple perfect scores to overcome, making recovery from early struggles nearly impossible. Third, traditional systems rarely provide opportunities to demonstrate improved understanding after initial poor performance, forcing students to move forward despite gaps in foundational knowledge. These practices disproportionately harm first-generation students, students of color, working students, and others who may need flexible timelines or multiple attempts to achieve mastery.

## Solution
Mastery grading assesses students solely on their demonstrated understanding of defined learning outcomes, typically allowing multiple attempts to demonstrate proficiency. Rather than accumulating points across diverse assignments, students are evaluated on whether they have achieved specific, clearly-articulated learning goals.

The approach involves three core elements:

**Proficiency-Based Scales:** Replace 0-100% scales with simpler rubrics (e.g., 0-4) that describe levels of understanding rather than percentages. Each level clearly defines what constitutes mastery versus partial understanding.

**Multiple Assessment Opportunities:** Build in regular reassessment opportunities so students can demonstrate improved understanding after receiving feedback and doing additional work. Initial low scores don't permanently damage final grades if students later achieve mastery.

**Separation of Content from Behaviors:** Evaluate content mastery separately from behaviors like attendance or timeliness. Late work may be accepted without penalty since the goal is determining whether students eventually master content, not whether they learned it on a predetermined schedule.

## Applicability
This approach works well when:
- Teaching courses with clearly definable, hierarchical learning outcomes (STEM courses, skills-based courses)
- Students need to master foundational concepts before advancing to dependent material
- You want to reduce stress and promote growth mindset
- You can manage the logistics of tracking multiple outcomes per student and offering reassessment
- Class sizes allow for individualized feedback (or you have TA support)

This approach may not work when:
- Learning goals are subjective or difficult to operationally define
- Class sizes are very large without TA support (tracking individual outcome mastery becomes unwieldy)
- Students lack self-direction skills to manage their own learning timelines
- Course doesn't have clear prerequisite relationships between topics

**Tradeoffs:** Requires significant upfront work to define granular learning outcomes and create multiple assessment versions. Ongoing logistics of tracking individual outcome mastery and managing reassessment sessions can be complex. However, grading individual assessments becomes faster with simpler rubrics, and the system better supports struggling students who need more time to learn.

## How to Implement

**Define Learning Outcomes at Multiple Levels**
Use backward design to identify:
- Big Ideas: overarching concepts spanning the entire course (4-6 outcomes)
- Module Outcomes: specific objectives for each unit/chapter (3-5 per module)
- Skills Outcomes: general academic skills relevant across the course

Write outcomes as assessable statements with clear verbs (calculate, identify, analyze, explain). Example: "Calculate energy from frequency and wavelength" rather than "Understand wave-particle duality."

**Create a Proficiency Scale**
Develop a simple scale (0-4 recommended) with clear descriptors for each level. The source uses:
- 4: Exceeded standard (A-level work, no errors)
- 3: Met standard (B-level proficiency, minor errors only)
- 2: Key gaps in understanding (C-level, basic understanding but mistakes on complex problems)
- 1: Cannot demonstrate proficiency without assistance (D-level)
- 0: Little/no evidence of understanding (F-level or no submission)

Share this rubric explicitly with students so expectations are clear.

**Design Multiple Assessment Touchpoints**
Assess each outcome at least 2-3 times through different formats:
- Initial assessment (quiz or homework)
- Application assessment (problem set or project)
- Summative assessment (unit exam)

Use later assessments to allow students to demonstrate improved mastery. The source used a "decaying average" for Big Ideas (recent scores weighted more heavily), explicitly promoting growth mindset.

**Establish Reassessment Procedures**
Create structured opportunities for students to reassess outcomes where they haven't achieved mastery:
- Schedule dedicated reassessment sessions (e.g., 2 weeks after each unit exam)
- Use final exam period as comprehensive reassessment opportunity
- Require prerequisite work (submitted application sets) before reassessing
- Create equivalent but different assessment questions for each outcome

**Modify Late Work Policies**
Since the goal is demonstrating mastery regardless of timeline, accept late work without grade penalties. Consider requiring something submitted by due dates to maintain accountability while still allowing flexibility. Distinguish between flexible deadlines (homework, projects) and fixed deadlines (in-class quizzes, exams).

**Calculate Final Grades from Outcome Mastery**
Average outcome scores within each category, then weight categories to calculate final grades. The source used:
- Modules: 60%
- Big Ideas: 20%
- Project: 10%
- Skills: 10%

Convert the resulting 0-4 score to letter grades (e.g., 3.7+ = A, 3.0-3.3 = B, etc.).

**Communicate Clearly with Students**
Explain the system thoroughly at course start. Many students are unfamiliar with mastery grading and need explicit guidance on how it differs from traditional grading and how to navigate the reassessment process.

## Examples

**Example 1: Chemistry Module Outcome Assessment**
Module 2 outcome: "Identify numbers of protons, neutrons, and electrons in isotopes and ions"

Assessment 1 (Quiz): Student scores 2/4 (can do simple ions but struggles with isotopes)
Assessment 2 (Problem Set): Student scores 3/4 (proficient but makes minor notation errors)
Assessment 3 (Unit Exam): Student scores 4/4 (full mastery demonstrated)

Final outcome score: 4/4 (most recent demonstration of mastery)

**Example 2: Grading Scale Impact**
Traditional 0-100% system: Student earns 0% on first assignment, then 90% on next five assignments. Average: (0+90+90+90+90+90)/6 = 75% (C)

Mastery 0-4 system: Student earns 0 on first assessment, then 3.7 on next five. Average: (0+3.7+3.7+3.7+3.7+3.7)/6 = 3.1 (B)

The single zero has less mathematical impact in the 0-4 system.

**Example 3: Flexible Deadlines Supporting Learning**
Student quote from source: "It allowed me to understand the content at my own pace rather than having to learn the content at the professor's pace, which in turn helped me learn the content better all together."

Another student: "I have taken chemistry 3 times and failed three times. This system of outcomes and mastery has not only allowed me to succeed in my most hated subject but to also actually enjoy it."

**Example 4: Retention Over Performance**
Student feedback: "While scoring well is a nice ego boost, it's meaningless if the retention of the material is shallow. Mastery-based outcomes force you to be evaluated on the same things if you haven't demonstrated proficiency, which is necessary given that engineers have a pretty important responsibility to know what they're doing in the field."

**Example 5: Reassessment Schedule**
Fall 2022: All reassessment happened during final exam week → overwhelming demand, scheduling chaos

Spring 2023 iteration: Dedicated reassessment sessions 2 weeks after Unit 1 and Unit 2 exams, plus final exam period for comprehensive reassessment → better distributed workload, earlier mastery of foundational content
