## Intent

Mastery grading is an equitable assessment model that prioritizes the demonstration of proficiency over the accumulation of points, allowing students to learn on their own timeline and be evaluated solely on their understanding of core course concepts.

## Problem

Conventional grading systems often penalize students for the time it takes them to learn. When assessments are fixed-point-in-time judgments with no opportunity for retakes, struggling students are forced to move on to advanced material with unaddressed knowledge gaps. Furthermore, traditional systems often conflate academic achievement with "compliance behaviors"—such as attendance, participation, or meeting strict deadlines—which disproportionately privileges students who already know how to "do school" while obscuring true content mastery.

## Solution

Shift the assessment focus to specific, transparent learning outcomes categorized into distinct levels of mastery. Use a coarse 0–4 scale (where 3 represents meeting the standard and 4 exceeds it) to replace the high-variance 100-point scale. This model decouples behavioral penalties from academic marks by eliminating late fees and allowing for flexible submission windows. Most importantly, it integrates a robust reassessment cycle where students can repeatedly demonstrate proficiency on specific outcomes through quizzes, projects, and exams until they reach the required level of mastery, ensuring that their final grade reflects their ultimate competency.

## Applicability

This play is highly effective for scaffolded STEM courses, such as engineering chemistry or mathematics, where prerequisite knowledge is critical for success in later units. It is particularly beneficial for first-generation and international students who may need more flexibility to navigate institutional norms. A significant tradeoff is the instructor time required for backwards course design and the management of multiple assessment versions. While it reduces "point-grubbing" and student anxiety, it requires students to have a baseline level of executive function to manage their own learning pace in a lower-supervision environment.

## How to Implement

Start by using backwards course design to identify three tiers of outcomes: granular module skills, cross-cutting "Big Ideas," and general academic skills. Define a 0–4 rubric for each outcome and share it in the syllabus so students understand exactly what "mastery" looks like. Organize the term into units where each outcome is assessed at least twice—initially via a low-stakes quiz and later through a module exam. For "Big Ideas," use a decaying average calculation that weights the most recent performance more heavily. Establish specific "reassessment windows" or office hour sessions where students can re-attempt failed outcomes without penalty. Finally, calculate the course grade as a weighted average of these category scores, ensuring that the final mark is a transparent reflection of the student's best and most recent demonstrations of knowledge.
