## Intent

Adapt Joe Feldman's "Grading for Equity" principles specifically for post-secondary Computer Science (CS) curricula, prioritizing project-based mastery, unbiased evaluation of group work, and the removal of punitive behavioral grading.

## Problem

While "Grading for Equity" provides a robust framework for K-12 education, higher education instructors—particularly in technical fields like Computer Science—often struggle to translate these principles into their courses. Challenges include the fundamental role of collaborative projects, the need for rigorous summative verification of individual skills, and the traditional reliance on high-stakes exams. Without a field-specific adaptation, CS instructors may inadvertently perpetuate inequities by penalizing students for learning-related behaviors (like late work) rather than focusing on their ultimate ability to write and understand code.

## Solution

Implement an integrated approach that centers the final course grade on significant project work while maintaining individual accountability through verified exams. This model replaces "effort-graded" daily tasks with simplified in-class activities and unlimited-attempt practice quizzes that serve as purely formative learning tools. To ensure fairness in collaborative work, use a "group grade adjusted by peer-evaluation" mechanism, where individual scores are scaled based on documented contributions. This system is underpinned by mathematically accurate policies—such as a 50% minimum grade floor and the elimination of late penalties—which maintain student motivation and allow for grade repair throughout the term.

## Applicability

This play is particularly valuable for CS educators in small-to-medium residential colleges, especially those serving students with neurodivergent learning profiles (e.g., ADHD, ASD, dyslexia). It is highly effective for project-intensive courses like Data Structures, Software Engineering, or Data Visualization. A primary tradeoff is the intensive instructor time required for qualitative peer-evaluation reviews and the design of multiple assessment versions. While tested in small classes (under 15), larger sections may require automated tools for peer-evaluation data processing and quiz management to remain scalable.

## How to Implement

Start by revising your syllabus to remove all points for "effort," "participation," or "extra credit." Set the project weight to at least 50% of the final grade and design a series of 3–5 formative quizzes for each module that students can retake until they achieve a perfect score. For group projects, implement a formal peer-evaluation system using a "Contribution Percentage" form; adjust each student's individual project score by multiplying the group grade by their verified contribution factor. To protect against early-semester failures, configure your gradebook to use 50% as the lowest possible score for any high-stakes attempt. Finally, ensure that every summative exam includes at least one significant question related to the student's project work, thereby individualizing the verification of their collaborative mastery.
