## Intent
Final grades weight formative assessments at 60% or more and summative assessments at 40% or less to compensate for traditional grading scale imbalances.

## Problem
Traditional 100-point grading systems create a mathematical inequity: 60% of possible scores (0-59) result in failure, while only 40% (60-100) represent passing grades. This imbalance makes it mathematically difficult for students to recover from early mistakes—a single zero requires nine perfect scores to repair. When students come from diverse backgrounds with different preparation levels, resources, and time availability, this system disproportionately impacts those who need more time to develop mastery, effectively punishing the learning process itself rather than measuring final achievement.

## Solution
This approach establishes explicit formative and summative assessment categories with strategic weighting that compensates for traditional grading scale imbalances. Formative assessments (designed for learning, with flexible deadlines and unlimited resubmissions) constitute at least 60% of the final grade, while summative assessments (designed to measure demonstrated competency at specific points) constitute no more than 40%.

The 60% threshold for formative work allows all students to reach passing grades through effort and persistence, regardless of starting point. The 40% summative weight then determines grade level within the passing range (D through A) based on demonstrated mastery. This structure maintains rigor through summative assessments while providing equitable opportunities for students who need more time or attempts to master concepts.

## Applicability
This play is particularly effective in contexts where instructors want to implement equitable grading practices but must report final grades using institutional 100-point scales. It works well when combined with other practices like flexible deadlines and resubmissions for formative work.

The approach requires that formative assessments genuinely support learning through features like multiple attempts and flexible deadlines. Simply relabeling traditional assignments as "formative" without changing their structure will not achieve the intended equity benefits.

This weighting strategy may need adjustment in courses with external certification requirements or accreditation standards that mandate specific percentages for particular assessment types. Instructors should also consider whether a 60/40 split or an even more formative-heavy split (like 70/30) better serves their course goals and student population.

## How to Implement

**Categorize all assessments as formative or summative.** Review your course assessments and designate each as either formative (designed to promote learning) or summative (designed to measure achievement at a point in time). Formative assessments should include items like weekly quizzes, practice problems, homework sets, lab assignments, and preliminary drafts. Summative assessments typically include final exams, final projects, practical demonstrations, and major papers.

**Calculate current weights and identify the split.** Total the percentage weight currently assigned to formative versus summative assessments. If formative work constitutes less than 60% of the final grade, you'll need to redistribute weights.

**Adjust weights to achieve at least 60/40 split.** Increase the weight of formative assessment categories and decrease summative categories until formative work represents at least 60% of the final grade. For example, in CS0 courses, a 70/30 split was used with formative assessments including interactive readings, coding labs, weekly knowledge checks, and formative essays at 70%, and summative assessments including midterm exam, final exam, and final practical project at 30%. In CS1 courses, a 60/40 split included formative assessments (knowledge checks, labs, formative exams, ungraded readings) at 60% and summative assessments (final exam, coding exams, reflective writing, final project) at 40%.

**Ensure formative assessments support learning through structure.** Configure formative assessments with features that enable their learning function: unlimited or high resubmission limits, flexible deadlines (recommended due dates with semester-end closing dates), and grading focused on completion or eventual mastery rather than first-attempt performance. Without these features, the weighting alone will not achieve equity goals.

**Communicate the rationale to students.** Explain that formative assessments are opportunities to learn through practice and mistakes, and that earning high formative grades through persistence ensures passing the course. Clarify that summative assessments demonstrate their final skill level and determine whether they pass with a D, C, B, or A. This transparency helps students understand how to approach different assessment types strategically.

## Examples

**CS0 (Python) with 70/30 split:**
- **Formative (70%):** Interactive readings and autograded coding labs (unlimited resubmissions), weekly knowledge checks (must score 3 or 4 to unlock next week's materials), and 3 of 4 essays (resubmitted after TA consultation)
- **Summative (30%):** Midterm exam, final exam, and final practical project with both coding and written components

**CS1 (Java) with 60/40 split:**
- **Formative (60%):** Weekly knowledge checks, coding labs, 3 of 4 exams (retakes allowed after earning 100% on readings, 100% on knowledge checks, and 80% on labs in the unit), and ungraded but tracked interactive reading assignments
- **Summative (40%):** Final exam, multiple coding exams, reflective writing assignments, and multi-week final project

**Key weighting principle:** A student earning 100% on all formative work (60%) but only 50% on summative assessments (20% out of 40%) would earn 80% overall—a B grade. This demonstrates how the weighting allows students to pass through learning effort while summative performance determines their specific passing grade level.
