## Intent

Use mastery or proficiency-based grading to simplify the assessment lifecycle, enabling instructors to create, grade, and manage summative assessments faster while shifting the focus from points to student learning.

## Problem

Traditional high-stakes assessments are labor-intensive for both students and educators. Instructors must spend significant time carefully assigning point values, designing exhaustive rubrics for partial credit, and pre-testing versions for security. This "scarcity of points" encourages student anxiety and cheating, while forcing educators into an adversarial relationship as "gatekeepers of points." Furthermore, students are often discouraged from attempting assessments if they are unsure of their success, leading to missed assignments and a lack of clear data on student progress.

## Solution

Shift to a mastery-based model that treats assessments as opportunities for feedback and growth rather than one-time judgments. By removing the emphasis on points and partial credit, assessments become lower-stakes diagnostic tools. A ternary scoring system (e.g., Mastered, Proficient, Not Yet Proficient) replaces fine-grained numerical grades, allowing instructors to provide binary feedback on whether criteria were met. This system encourages students to attempt every assessment without fear of permanent penalty, as they know they can revise and retake specific learning targets until they reach the required level of mastery.

## Applicability

This approach is particularly effective in technical or math-based disciplines where learning targets are clearly defined. It requires a robust revision policy and the availability of multiple versions of an assessment (or similar problems) to ensure that students engage with new material during retakes. While it significantly reduces the time spent on initial grading and rubric design, instructors must account for the logistical tradeoff of managing retakes and tracking progress across multiple attempts. It works best in environments where students are motivated by clear expectations and the opportunity for redemption.

## How to Implement

Start by defining clear, measurable learning targets for each module. When grading, instead of writing extensive individual feedback, simply circle where a solution begins to deviate from expectations and mark it as "Not Yet Proficient." Use a class-wide review to address common mistakes identified during grading. For borderline work, default to requiring a retake to ensure complete understanding. To save time, only create new problems for the specific targets students failed to master, rather than re-versioning entire exams. Finally, maintain a transparent tracking system (such as a proficiency table) so students can monitor their own progress toward course-level mastery without the confusion of fluctuating point totals.
