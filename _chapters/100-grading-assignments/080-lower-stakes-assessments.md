## Intent
Summative assessments become faster for educators to create and grade when using mastery or proficiency-based grading systems.

## Problem
Traditional points-based grading without revision opportunities creates high-stakes assessments for both students and educators. For students, the pressure to maximize points on a single attempt can lead to test anxiety, avoidance of assessments when uncertain, and increased cheating as they calculate risk versus reward. For educators and assessment designers, high-stakes assessments demand extensive time investment: carefully calibrating point values, developing detailed rubrics for all forms of partial credit, pre-testing to eliminate ambiguities, creating multiple exam versions for security, grading with extreme precision to ensure fairness, and analyzing score distributions for consistency across instructors.

## Solution
Mastery-based and other equitable grading systems incorporate revision and retake opportunities that fundamentally change how assessments function. When students can revise work or retake assessments, individual attempts shift from high-stakes evaluations to lower-stakes learning opportunities. This reframing eliminates many time-consuming assessment design requirements while maintaining student motivation—students still want to perform well on first attempts to identify what they know and what requires improvement before meeting final expectations.

Because students can recover from early mistakes through subsequent attempts, assessment creation no longer requires exhaustive pre-testing for ambiguities (students can simply attempt revised problems), point calibration becomes less critical (or unnecessary if using binary or standards-based evaluation), rubrics can focus on whether revision is needed rather than precise partial credit allocation, and version security concerns decrease substantially as student discussion becomes part of the learning process rather than a threat to assessment integrity.

## Applicability
This approach works effectively when instructors can provide revision or retake opportunities for assessments. It functions particularly well in courses where learning objectives can be assessed through multiple equivalent problems or tasks, making it feasible to create alternative versions or revision activities.

The system requires planning for how students will revise or demonstrate improved mastery. Because students will naturally discuss assessment results with peers (which becomes productive learning rather than academic dishonesty), instructors need different problems for subsequent attempts or alternative revision activities rather than identical retakes.

This approach may require adjustment in contexts where external constraints limit revision opportunities (such as courses with mandated assessment schedules), or where creating multiple equivalent assessment versions presents significant challenges due to limited question banks or highly specialized content.

## How to Implement

**Redesign assessment creation around learning rather than point distribution.** Instead of calibrating point values and developing exhaustive partial credit rubrics, focus on creating problems that clearly assess whether students can demonstrate the targeted skills or knowledge. Assessment creation becomes faster because problems only need to assess the learning objective—not fit specific point quotas or balance difficulty to achieve target score distributions.

**Establish clear criteria for what demonstrates mastery versus what needs revision.** Rather than detailed rubrics assigning partial credit, determine what constitutes acceptable evidence of understanding. For mastery-based systems, this might be binary (meets expectations or doesn't), or use a simple scale like "Mastered," "Proficient," or "Not yet proficient." For borderline work, default to requiring revision—the additional practice benefits student learning.

**Simplify grading to focus on feedback for revision.** Grade by identifying where solutions begin to go wrong rather than calculating precise point deductions. Circle the error location and move on. Note common mistakes across all student work to share with the class when returning assessments, but avoid writing extensive individualized feedback on each paper (students will discuss solutions with peers and can ask questions in class or office hours if still unclear). Since grading is not point-based, perfect consistency across all papers becomes less critical—students who receive different feedback can both revise their work for full credit without fairness concerns.

**Reduce or eliminate exam security measures that consume time.** The reduced incentive to cheat (students can revise for full credit) means less need for multiple exam versions. If a version has a typo or unclear question, affected students simply solve replacement problems on their revision without complex fairness negotiations about point adjustments.

**Create straightforward revision opportunities.** Students retake only the specific learning objectives they didn't master, making subsequent grading faster than initial attempts. Design revision processes that don't require extensive instructor time—for example, students might solve new problems during office hours or designated retake sessions rather than full class periods.

**For borderline or revision-needed work, require the revision attempt.** Don't spend time determining whether work barely misses expectations or barely meets them—if uncertain, mark for revision. The repetition benefits learning, and the binary decision (acceptable/needs revision) is faster than calibrating precise scores.

## Examples

**BC Calculus with mastery/proficiency levels (Trimm):** Assessments evaluated student work as "Mastered" (meets all high-level expectations), "Proficient" (demonstrates understanding of more basic problems or significant progress on complex problems), or "Not yet proficient." Each assessment tested specific learning targets (e.g., "I can find limits using the Squeeze Theorem"). Students who didn't reach mastery on specific targets took retakes one week later during midday study time—solving different problems of similar difficulty for those specific targets only. Creating assessments became faster because problems only needed to assess the learning targets without careful point calibration, and grading took just minutes per class: "each solution either meets the expectations or it doesn't. I just circle where the solution started to go wrong, and then move on. DO NOT write feedback on the paper - this is very time-consuming and mostly unnecessary. I just note any common mistakes, which I point out to the class when I hand them back."

**Cell Biology with 20 learning outcomes (Katzman et al.):** Students received scores of "Mastered," "Proficient," or "Not yet proficient" on quizzes assessing individual learning outcomes. Mastery required achieving 80% or higher on the assessment problems for that specific outcome. If students didn't master content on their first attempt, they earned retake opportunities by completing assignments associated with that learning outcome's content (worksheets, online quizzes, or summaries). The pass/fail nature of LO assessment eliminated the need for precise point rubrics—instructors simply determined whether the 80% threshold was met. Class size was capped at 24 students, allowing individualized feedback on each attempt without the time burden of calculating and justifying partial credit.

**Statistics problem sets:** Students submitted answers to all problems and discussed solutions in randomly assigned three-person groups. Full credit was awarded for completing the work and participating in discussion—independent of correctness. After group discussions, the instructor provided correct answers and responded to questions. The same concepts appeared later on a final exam graded for quality. Creating these assessments required only gathering appropriate problems without calibrating point values, and grading focused solely on completion verification rather than evaluating correctness of each step.

**Derivative calculations assessment:** A checkpoint included five functions requiring derivative computation using multiple rules in combination. Rather than assigning points to each function or each step within the solution, the instructor evaluated each function as meeting mastery expectations (correct method, correct answer with at most 1-2 insignificant errors like sign mistakes) or needing revision. Students marked for revision solved different functions using the same derivative rules on the retake. This binary evaluation was substantially faster than traditional grading that would require determining how many points to deduct for each type of error and ensuring consistency across all student papers.
