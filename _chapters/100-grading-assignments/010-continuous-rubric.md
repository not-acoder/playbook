## Intent

Improve matrix-style rubrics by using a 'continua model' that depicts standards as overlapping zones along an arrow of increasing quality, making assessment judgments more visual and less prone to 'grid-filling' errors.

## Problem

Matrix-style rubrics often force instructors into 'all or nothing' boxes, assuming each step across standards represents an equal 'quantum leap' in quality. This creates pressure to manufacture descriptors for every cell ('filling the grid') even when criteria don't naturally fit that structure. The resulting ambiguity makes it difficult for students to see the path toward improvement and for multiple assessors to achieve consistency on borderline cases.

## Solution

The Continua Model of a Guide to Making Judgments (GTMJ) replaces discrete boxes with achievement arrows. It employs four core principles: (1) Standards are represented as continuous arrows rather than discrete boxes; (2) Variable descriptors are used, including only the number necessary to show progression; (3) Descriptors are 'nested,' meaning a higher-level behavior implicitly includes lower-level behaviors to reduce repetitive text; and (4) Success is depicted as a 'region of quality' or zone along the arrow, allowing for 'on-balance' judgments rather than requiring every descriptor be met for a fixed level.

## Applicability

This play is highly effective in standards-based or specifications grading contexts where learning outcomes are complex and multifaceted. It works particularly well in teacher education or professional programs where assessors possess tacit knowledge of 'quality' but need a tool to articulate it consistently. Tradeoffs include an initial time investment to rethink rubric language into a nested structure. It may be less suitable for tasks with very narrow, objective right/wrong criteria where a simple checklist suffices.

## How to Implement

1. Identify the core learning outcomes for the assignment. 2. For each outcome, draft descriptors that capture only the *key distinguishing differences* as quality increases, rather than repeating all behaviors in every level. 3. Map these descriptors onto a continuous arrow, ensuring that each higher descriptor implicitly includes those below it ('nestedness'). 4. Define 'standard zones' along the arrow that represent ranges of quality (e.g., Proficient, Advanced) rather than fixed points. 5. Train assessors to make an 'on-balance' judgment by placing a tick anywhere along the arrow where the student's work most accurately lands, facilitating easier moderation of borderline submissions.
