## Intent
Assign grades based on student growth relative to their individual starting point rather than fixed performance benchmarks.

## Problem
Traditional rubrics evaluate all students against the same performance standards, regardless of starting ability. This creates a demotivating dynamic where struggling students who work hard and improve significantly still receive low grades because they haven't reached benchmark proficiency, while high-performing students receive good grades with minimal effort because they already meet expectations. Students often "settle into their place on the rubric and get stuck there with very little variation, even when the performance task itself changes," resulting in perpetual frustration for low-performers and passive boredom for high-performers whose potential quietly evaporates.

## Solution
Slide rubrics individualize grading by basing letter grades on growth rather than absolute performance. The first assessment establishes each student's baseline position on an expanded rubric (typically 9 levels instead of the traditional 4-6). Subsequent assessments award grades based on movement up or down from that individual baseline—not based on the rubric level achieved.

For example, a student starting at level 3 who performs at level 4 receives a B for demonstrating growth, while a student starting at level 5 who performs at level 4 receives a D for declining performance—even though both produced work of identical quality. The grade reflects whether the student learned and improved during the unit, not whether they met a universal standard.

The expanded rubric typically ranges from "utterly rudimentary" (levels 1-2) through "partially proficient" (levels 3-4), "proficient" (level 5), to "near professional performance" (levels 7-9), providing enough granularity to meaningfully track individual growth across diverse ability levels.

## Applicability
This approach works effectively when courses include multiple assessments of the same skills over time and when the primary educational goal is student growth rather than certification of competency at a fixed standard.

The system is particularly valuable in contexts with wide variation in student preparation levels, where traditional grading leaves struggling students perpetually discouraged and advanced students unchallenged. It functions well in middle and high school settings where motivating continued growth matters more than absolute performance benchmarks.

This approach requires adjustment in contexts where external standards mandate specific competency levels (such as college entrance requirements or professional certification) or where parents and administrators expect grades to reflect absolute performance against grade-level benchmarks. In these contexts, slide rubrics might supplement rather than replace traditional grading, or be paired with clear communication about how rubric scores differ from letter grades.

The system works best when students will have the same instructor across multiple assessments, as it requires tracking individual baselines and growth trajectories over time.

## How to Implement

**Establish baseline positions through pretesting.** Before implementing slide rubrics, assess students on the target skill to establish each student's starting position on the rubric. This initial assessment uses traditional evaluation—all students measured against the same standard. The baseline score determines where each student starts on the slide rubric for subsequent assessments.

**Design an expanded rubric with 9 levels instead of 4-6.** Traditional rubrics typically use 4-6 performance levels (such as Unsatisfactory, Partially Proficient, Proficient, Advanced). Expand this to approximately 9 levels that span from "utterly rudimentary" through "near professional performance." This wider range accommodates the full spectrum of student abilities and provides enough granularity to meaningfully measure growth. Include "between" levels that describe performance falling between major categories.

For example, an essay rubric might define level 1 as "No introduction attempted," level 2 as "Introduction will be very brief (one or two sentences) and very basic," level 5 as "Introduction fills a complete paragraph and cleanly outlines the major points of the essay," and level 9 as "Introduction is clear, direct, creative and very engaging to the reader." Each criterion on the rubric (introduction, thesis, evidence, organization, etc.) receives its own 9-level scale.

**Assign individualized grading scales based on baseline performance.** After establishing each student's baseline position, create personalized grading scales where staying at their baseline earns a C, moving up one level earns a B, moving up two or more levels earns an A, moving down one level earns a D, and moving down two or more levels earns an F.

For Mike at baseline level 3: level 3 = C, level 4 = B, level 5+ = A, level 2 = D, level 1 = F.
For Clarissa at baseline level 5: level 5 = C, level 6 = B, level 7+ = A, level 4 = D, level 3 or below = F.

This means Mike and Clarissa can both produce level 4 work, but Mike earns a B (for improving from level 3) while Clarissa earns a D (for declining from level 5).

**Adjust baseline positions when students consistently exceed their level.** Once students begin consistently scoring two levels above their official position, shift their baseline up by one level (or two in exceptional cases). This elevates the challenge and creates opportunities for continued growth. The adjustment prevents students from remaining at an artificially low baseline that no longer challenges them.

**Prepare students and parents with transparent communication about the system.** Before the first slide rubric assessment, explain clearly how the grading works, explicitly noting that "within this system, one paper may actually look worse than another and still receive a higher grade" because one student demonstrated growth while the other showed no learning. Students typically understand this quickly when you frame it as recognizing actual learning rather than innate ability.

Frame the conversation around fairness: "If I asked you to write a single paragraph, some could do this in minutes with minimal effort while others would take an entire class period and still produce lower quality. Who actually worked harder? Who grew? While high performance is always the goal, growth is actually the measure that really matters."

Note that parents have been "universally supportive" when this rationale is explained, as the focus on individual progress resonates with their desire for their children to improve regardless of starting point.

**Make proficiency levels visible alongside letter grades.** Even though letter grades reflect growth, students still need to see their actual proficiency level relative to standards. Show both the rubric score (which indicates actual performance level) and the letter grade (which indicates growth from baseline). This transparency ensures students understand where they stand relative to grade-level expectations while being rewarded for improvement.

**Focus instruction on individual student needs based on their rubric positions.** Use the individualized baselines to differentiate instruction. Students below proficiency receive targeted support on foundational skills they need to advance. Students at proficiency work on maintaining and refining their performance. Advanced students receive enriched challenges that push them toward near-professional performance. The slide rubric creates natural groupings for tailored instruction during class time.

## Examples

**Mike and Clarissa's essays (Aguire):** Mike starts at level 3 on a 9-level essay rubric. When he scores a 3, he receives a C. He works hard and writes better than ever before, achieving a rubric score of 4, earning him a B. Clarissa is a strong writer set at level 5. She rushes through the assignment and scores only a 4 on the rubric, earning her a D. Even though Mike's essay (level 4) is roughly the same quality as Clarissa's essay (level 4), Mike earned a B and Clarissa earned a D based on their different starting positions. Mike demonstrated significant learning during the unit; Clarissa demonstrated no learning at all. One is growing, the other is slacking.

**9-level essay introduction rubric (Aguire):** The expanded rubric for essay introductions defines:
- Level 1: "No introduction attempted"
- Level 2: "Introduction will be very brief (one or two sentences) and very basic"
- Level 5: "Introduction fills a complete paragraph and cleanly outlines the major points of the essay"
- Level 7: "Introduction not only cleanly outlines the major points of the essay, but makes a clear attempt to get the reader's attention with at least a little success"
- Level 9: "Introduction is clear, direct, creative and very engaging to the reader"

Levels 3, 4, 6, and 8 are defined as "Between" levels that fall between these major categories. This granularity allows meaningful differentiation across a wide range of student abilities.

**Adjusting baseline after consistent growth (Aguire):** A student officially positioned at level 3 begins consistently scoring at level 5 (two levels above their baseline). The instructor shifts that student's baseline up by one level to level 4. This adjustment elevates the challenge for that student, requiring them to reach level 5 to earn a C and level 6 to earn a B, creating opportunities for new growth rather than allowing them to coast on previous improvements.

**Pretest to establish initial positions (Aguire):** Before implementing the slide rubric system for a writing unit, the instructor assigns an initial essay that all students complete. This essay is graded using a traditional rubric where everyone is measured against the same standard. A student who scores at level 2 is positioned at level 2 for subsequent assessments. A student who scores at level 6 is positioned at level 6. From these positions forward, all grades reflect movement up or down from these baselines rather than absolute performance.

**Explaining the system to students (Aguire):** After the pretest, the instructor has a straightforward conversation: "In this system, two papers of the same quality might receive different grades. Here's why: If I asked you to write a paragraph, some of you could do it in minutes with minimal effort and produce great work. Others would spend the entire class period and still produce something of lower quality. Who worked harder? Who actually learned something? While everyone's goal is high performance, your grade measures whether you're better than you were before—not whether you're as good as someone else. There's no shame in being at level 2 right now—lots of people are. What matters is whether you move forward."

**Balancing growth and standards (Aguire):** An instructor shows both rubric scores and letter grades on returned work. A student receives their paper marked "Rubric Score: 4, Grade: B." The rubric score shows their actual performance level relative to standards. The letter grade shows they improved from their baseline of 3. This transparency ensures students understand both where they stand relative to grade-level expectations AND that they're being recognized for growth. The instructor can then point toward proficiency (level 5) as the ongoing goal: "You're making great progress—you've moved from level 3 to level 4. Keep working on [specific skill] to reach proficiency at level 5."
