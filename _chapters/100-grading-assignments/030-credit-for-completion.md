---
title: Credit for Completion, not Quality
slug: credit-for-completion
---
## Intent
Students receive full credit for completing learning activities regardless of performance quality.

## Problem
Traditional grading systems that reward performance quality on every task can heighten student anxiety and discourage the extensive practice necessary for skill development. When students fear poor grades, they avoid attempting new problems voluntarily, limiting their opportunities to develop competence and confidence through practice.

## Solution
This approach awards grades based on completion of learning activities rather than performance quality. Students receive full credit for submitting complete work and discussing their answers with peers, independent of correctness. The instructor then provides correct answers and responds to questions during or after peer discussions. This creates a low-stakes environment where students can practice skills, make mistakes, and build confidence before being evaluated on performance quality (typically on exams or final assessments).
## Applicability
This play works well in courses where student anxiety is high and extensive practice is essential for skill development, such as introductory statistics, mathematics, or science courses. It is particularly effective for building foundational skills and confidence in courses where students have postponed enrollment due to fear of the subject matter.

The approach may be less suitable for advanced courses requiring detailed performance feedback to refine complex skills, or contexts with strict performance metrics for certification or licensure.

The system is vulnerable to two forms of potential abuse: students may submit low-effort work just to earn points (the author estimates fewer than 5-10 percent do this), and instructors seeking to minimize grading time may neglect providing the constructive feedback that makes this system effective.

## How to Implement

**Identify completion-based activities.** Select learning activities where practice matters more than initial correctness—such as problem sets, observations, field projects, or reflection papers. These should be activities where students benefit from attempting the work without performance pressure.

**Define clear completion criteria.** Specify exactly what constitutes "completion" for each activity type:
- For problem sets: Submission of answers to all questions plus participation in small-group discussion of answers
- For observations: Conducting the observation for the specified duration and submitting a written record
- For field projects: Describing specified components (objectives, assessments, concept applications, self-perceived strengths and weaknesses) regardless of teaching success or report quality
- For reaction papers: Submitting a reflection of any content, regardless of grammar, clarity, or expressed attitude

**Structure peer discussion.** Use randomly assigned small groups (e.g., three-person groups) where students discuss their answers before receiving correct solutions. The instructor circulates among groups to address remaining questions.

**Provide feedback after completion credit is earned.** After students have received credit for completion, supply correct answers, sample solutions, or detailed comments on their work. This separates the grade from the informational feedback, making students more likely to attend to the feedback itself rather than just the grade.

**Maintain performance-based assessments.** Reserve some course components (typically exams) for quality-based grading. Ensure that concepts practiced in completion-based activities reappear on these assessments, rewarding students who engaged meaningfully with the practice opportunities.

**Establish transparency and consistency.** Clearly communicate which activities are completion-based versus quality-based, what constitutes completion, and what percentage of the final grade each component represents. This clarity reduces anxiety and helps students understand the grading structure.

## Examples

**Statistics problem sets:** Students who submit answers to all questions on a problem set and later discuss their answers within a randomly assigned three-person group receive full credit. After small group discussions, the instructor provides correct answers and responds to questions. The same statistical concepts appear on the final exam, which is graded for quality.

**Take-home quizzes:** In an introductory education course, students receive a specified maximum number of points for submitting a completed take-home quiz and discussing answers in small groups. The instructor circulates to address remaining questions, then provides sample answers. Content is cumulative across quizzes, and the same concepts appear on the final exam graded for quality.

**Field teaching projects:** Students receive full credit for describing specific required elements of their out-of-class teaching experience: objectives and assessments used, attempted application of at least three course concepts, and self-perceptions of teaching strengths and weaknesses. Credit is not contingent upon teaching success or report quality, but the instructor provides extensive comments on student self-assessments.

**Observation assignments:** In a child development course, students earn full credit by conducting two fifteen-minute observations and submitting written records of those interactions. The instructor provides constructive feedback on all observations, with some portions discussed by the class as a whole.

**Reaction papers:** In a self-development course, students receive a specified number of points for writing a reaction paper to an experiential exercise, regardless of content, grammar, clarity, or expressed attitude.
