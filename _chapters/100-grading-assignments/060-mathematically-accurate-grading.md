## Intent

Remove the inaccuracies inherent in traditional numerical grading—such as the 100-point scale and mathematical averaging—to ensure final grades more accurately reflect student learning and end-of-course achievement.

## Problem

Traditional grading relies on a 0-100 scale that is mathematically flawed and biased against student recovery. The scale is too fine-grained, creating high variance where it is impossible to meaningfully distinguish between adjacent points (e.g., 78 vs 79). Furthermore, the 60-point "failing" range (0-59) disproportionately weights failure compared to the 10-point ranges for success, making it nearly impossible for a student to recover from a single low score. Standard averaging also hides a student's growth trajectory, penalizing those who take longer to reach proficiency and obscuring the fact that they may have achieved mastery by the end of the course.

## Solution

To achieve mathematical accuracy, instructors should adopt grading practices that emphasize current competency over historical averages. This includes replacing the 0-100 scale with a coarser 0-4 proficiency scale, which is cognitively easier to grasp and less prone to statistical error. If a 100-point scale must be maintained, a minimum grade (e.g., 50%) should be established to prevent catastrophic failures from "tanking" a student's entire grade. Additionally, indicators for missing work should be clearly separated from scores for incorrect work, and final grades should weight recent performance more heavily to reward the student's ultimate level of mastery rather than penalizing their initial struggles.

## Applicability

This play is widely applicable across disciplines and course levels, fitting both traditional and alternative grading models like standards-based or specifications grading. It is particularly valuable for supporting students from historically underserved backgrounds who may require more time or revision opportunities to reach proficiency. However, instructors should consider the tradeoff of potential administrative friction, as some institutions may have rigid policies regarding minimum grades or fixed weightings. It works best when students are frequently reminded that their grade is a reflection of their growth and current skills, not a permanent record of early mistakes.

## How to Implement

Implementation requires a shift in how scores are recorded and calculated, often necessitating creative workarounds for Course Management Systems (CMS). Instructors should start by defining a clear mapping between proficiency levels and numerical values (e.g., 4=Advanced, 3=Proficient). To manage CMS limitations, instructors can manually override final grade calculations to prioritize recent assessments or use the median/mode instead of the mean to mitigate the impact of outliers. For missing work, use an "incomplete" placeholder instead of a zero until the student demonstrates understanding. For those who prefer more transparency, providing a self-tracking grade sheet (e.g., a shared spreadsheet) allows students to see their progress in real-time and understand how their latest performance directly influences their standing, though this requires students to maintain their own records consistently.
