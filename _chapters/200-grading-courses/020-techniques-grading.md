## Intent

Techniques Grading (TG) is a mastery-based system for proof-intensive mathematics courses that frames learning objectives as specific proof techniques, allowing students to demonstrate proficiency through iterative revisions and binary evaluations.

## Problem

Standard proof-based courses often struggle to balance the teaching of mathematical facts with the development of "habits of mind"—the analytical thinking required to construct rigorous proofs. Traditional grading can prioritize final outputs over the learning process, penalizing students for initial struggles with complex logic. This can discourage risk-taking and prevent students from truly internalizing the discipline-specific "tricks of the trade" necessary to think like an expert analyst, topologist, or algebraist.

## Solution

Implement a structured framework built on four pillars: binarity, product-focus, multiple chances, and transparency. In TG, technique objectives are defined as the mastery of specific, broadly applicable proof methods (e.g., induction, contradiction, or sequential characterization). Students are graded on their work product using a three-tier marking scheme: Satisfactory (S), Progressing (P), or Unsatisfactory (U). An 'S' indicates complete mastery, while a 'P' signals that the student has correctable flaws and is encouraged to revise and resubmit without penalty. This iterative feedback loop, combined with a transparent syllabus that maps every skill to a grade category, ensures that students focus on deep conceptual understanding rather than point accumulation.

## Applicability

This play is ideal for advanced undergraduate or graduate-level courses in pure mathematics (e.g., real analysis, abstract algebra, or combinatorics) where proof-writing is the primary skill. It is particularly effective for fostering a supportive relationship between instructor and student, as the "S/P/U" model simplifies grading to two binary questions: "Is it correct?" and "Can it be fixed?" A primary tradeoff is the initial complexity of the syllabus, which may require significant student buy-in and clear communication from day one. It also requires the instructor to carefully select a manageable number of technique objectives that appear frequently enough across assignments to provide students with multiple "at-bats."

## How to Implement

Start by identifying 15–30 core proof techniques from your course materials and categorize them into tiers (e.g., Level I and Level II) if appropriate. Divide your grading into three buckets: Technique Objectives, Exam Objectives (low-stakes recall of definitions), and Participation Objectives. To earn a letter grade, a student must meet the threshold for that grade in all categories (e.g., an 'A' requires 30 technique 'S' marks, 85% on exams, and 90% participation). Provide weekly proof assignments and grade them promptly using the S/P/U system; allow unlimited resubmissions for any 'P' marks. At the end of the term, require students to submit a final portfolio where they collate their 'S' proofs and provide a brief written reflection explaining how each proof demonstrates a specific technique. This ensures they can articulate their mastery before receiving their final grade.
